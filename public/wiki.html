First Steps

Make an account on the login screen (username + password — auto-saves locally so you only do this once). You'll start with 500 donuts and the free starter loadout: AK20, SG-8, Pistol, Flare, Fists, Frying Pan, Frag, Medkit. From the mode-select screen you can shop, redeem promo codes, or jump straight into a match.

🧟 Zombie SurvivalSolo, endless, and always on a map of its own: the Tunnel, one straight hall 72 m wide and 4.6 km long (about eighteen times the floor of an ordinary map, all in a line), in the dark: a thin moon, thick fog, a few lamps whose light follows you down it, and a small headlamp of your own. It is built from 44 m sections (pillar rows, staggered barricades, fallen rubble, a raised walkway with a ramp at each end, container yards, side alcoves, an open stretch) with a high checkpoint wall and a 12 m door every ten sections, and no other mode is ever dealt it. You start in the intact entrance hall at the south end and, after the fall, wake 260 m into the tunnel. The dead come out of the dark ahead of you and behind you, and the ones you leave far behind are let go; the HUD counts how deep you have got. Waves of zombies come for you, each bigger, tougher and quicker than the last (wave 1 is 7 zombies of 68 HP; never more than 26 on the map at once, and a wave that drags on is followed by the next one anyway). The dead have skins of their own that nobody can wear: plain green people in ordinary T-shirts (four colours, so a crowd is not one body), with two black dashes for eyes and a flat, downturned mouth, and nobody looks happy about it, and a claw that hits for 30 after a third of a second of wind-up, so stepping away dodges it. It opens with a story: you start with a squad (Reyes, Okafor, Brandt and Kim) walking into the place you were sent to raid; a few seconds in the floor caves in and the screen goes black. Then there is only static crackling, and each time it breaks a message comes up across the top of the screen with your own face beside it: you, recording what happened (the squad's lines come up the same way with their faces). The squad is lying dead around you, something is horribly wrong with this place, and you are the only one left. Then the lights come back and the first wave comes. About a minute and a half, and you can skip it (Enter or Space, or the SKIP button on a phone). You begin with only your fists (the Uppercut is the opening move). Every zombie has a small chance to drop a new weapon (3.5%, growing a little with every zombie that gave nothing) or ammo (6%, only if you carry something that uses it). What drops is not the whole armoury: pistols and revolvers, shotguns, SMGs, rifles and snipers, a few heavies (minigun, chain gun, rocket launcher), some melee (bat, knife, sabre, katana, axe, chainsaw, sledge) and a handful of throwables (grenade, molotov, medkit, stim, mine, turret), in four steps of rarity, never a gimmick gun and never anything from the archive. It goes in your second slot: walk over it to take it, and what you were holding is gone: it disappears, it is not left on the floor. Your fists always keep slot 1; keys 1 and 2 switch. Clearing a wave gives you 25 HP back. You do not respawn: it ends when they get you, and your best wave is kept.
🎲 Dice DuelYou and a bot each carry a d20, and you roll it during the round, not before it (G, or the 🎲 button on a phone). The die sets the tier (1 is junk, 20 is an SS), then a thing from it: a gun, a melee or a utility, from a pool that includes the retired gimmick weapons (see The RNG pool). You start each round with empty hands, and nothing from your loadout (no melee, no utility). You hold two things at once (keys 1 and 2): a roll goes in an empty slot, otherwise it replaces the one you are not holding and goes straight into your hand, so to throw a bad one away, switch to the good one and roll. Rolling costs nothing, but you cannot shoot while the dice rattle (0.7 s) and they need 5 s before they can be rolled again. The bot has the same dice (a gun at a time; it re-rolls when what it holds is worth less than a fresh roll, when the dice are ready and nobody has just hit it). First to 5 kills; every kill starts a fresh round with empty hands and a fresh dice.

🔪 Quick melee

B (or the middle mouse button) swings the melee in your pocket, or fires its ability if it is ready, and puts you back on your gun. If the ability is cooling down it swings instead of doing nothing, and an ability that takes a moment to play out (the Bat's Crowd Clear charge, a slam, a dash, the Battle Axe's Whirlwind, the Scythe's aimed dash) is allowed to finish before the gun comes back; they were being cancelled. Meteor Hammer (new): the maul had no ability. It now flings its weight 12 m on the chain, the way the Chinese meteor hammer was used. A person in the way takes 60, is wrapped and reeled in, and is held a moment; a wall, ceiling or the floor takes the hook and the chain hauls you there. Nothing in reach, and nothing is spent.

🔥💧 Fire, water and the flame jet

Weapons are meant to interact, so a few of them now answer each other.

MechanicHow it works
Flame jetFire the Flamethrower at the floor under you (aim at least 30° down, flames reaching the floor within 4.6 m) and every round shoves you up a little, to a cap of about 5.6 m/s. It runs on fuel: about two seconds of fire in the air, refilled quickly on the ground. The steeper you aim, the higher you can hover, since the flames have to reach the floor. It never interrupts a slam or a dash.
Water gunsThe Foam Cannon, and any gun in a skin that squirts or sprinkles (water pistol, fire extinguisher, lawn sprinkler, soap, slush). Firing one douses you (burning stops, and you cannot catch fire for 3 s; a burn zone only does a third of its heat to a wet player), puts out the burn zones and flares where the stream lands, and takes the fire off a bot standing in it.
SmokeA smoke cloud is wet too: burn zones and ground flares inside it go out, and anyone standing in it stops burning and cannot catch fire while they stay (plus a couple of seconds after).

Why: the Flamethrower and the Molotov were pure damage with no answer. Now there is one (spray yourself down, throw smoke on the fire), and the Flamethrower gets a movement trick of its own in return.

📨 Painted wraps, approval and weapon ideas

Anybody can paint a weapon wrap in the pixelizer. A wrap you painted is a draft: it can be previewed in the pixelizer but not worn. Press Submit for approval and it goes to the admins' queue (F2 → Wrap & idea queue); once one approves it, it is yours to use and everybody in your match sees it on your gun. A wrap that is not approved cannot be used, and nobody else sees it. Admins' own wraps are approved on the spot. You can have five waiting at once.

Everyone sees your wrap. Whichever wrap you have on (a built-in, a contract wrap or an approved one) is sent with you, and other players see it on the weapon in your hands. Character skins you paint are shared the same way they were before.

Weapon ideas. Gun Skins → Send a weapon idea sends a name and a few sentences to the admins, who read them from the same queue.

Third-person weapons. Other players (and bots, up to a limit) now hold the weapon's own model, not one of four stand-in shapes, so a chain gun looks like a chain gun. They aim up and down with where their owner is looking.

⚔️ Duel Maps (4) · 1v1 and 2v2

A 1v1 on a map built for ten people was mostly walking, and the first duel maps (36 × 56 m) turned out to be too tiny the other way, and so were the next ones (72 × 112 m). 1v1 now draws from four arenas of its own at 144 × 224 m, twice the length and the breadth again: the spawns are 200 m apart, about half a minute to the other side at a walk, less if you slide or take a ramp. Each map is the old arena laid in all four quarters (the second one turned over) plus pieces made for the whole map, and it is full of high walls: long walls 5.5 m tall (nothing is seen over them from the floor), at least 24 on every map and some in every quarter, one across the whole middle, and screens in front of each spawn. The build check counts them. A grid floor and flat colours, laid out rotationally symmetric (turn the map half a circle and it is the same map), so neither spawn has the better side. Every one has ramps up onto something worth standing on, and each is built from a different mix of walls (long walls, waist-high walls, windowed walls with a sill and a lintel, arches, L-shaped bunkers, crate stacks, decks), with pillars only a small part of one. They are dealt to 1v1 and to 2v2 now and then (they are on 2v2's vote screen and about three random 2v2 draws in ten land on one, since there is room for four); they never come up in anything else. You vote for the map once your opponent is found, not before (see Voting for the map): the same pick plays and two different picks are settled by a coin flip. A bot votes too: it reads a plan drawn from the map's real walls, ramps and spawns and judges how much cover there is, how long the lanes run and how much high ground there is, then picks the map its taste likes (an easy bot changes its mind more). Nobody voting in 15 seconds is a random map. You cannot see your opponent at the start: something solid and wider than the spawn stands across the middle of every one, and the build check fails if any line from one spawn to the other is clear.

👣 Hearing other people

Only your own footsteps used to be played, so on a 144 × 224 m duel map finding the other person was a long silent walk. Everyone else steps out loud now, and it works the way your ears would:

WhatHow it sounds
DistanceA step every 2.8 m walked (so a runner is quick and a creeper slow); louder near, gone past about 34 m.
DirectionPanned to the side the person is on, relative to where you face; duller when they are behind you.
WallsMuffled and half as loud through a wall, so you can tell they are near and not in view.
Crouching, sliding, landingCrouched is a third as loud (and slower steps); a slide makes no step; a drop is heard as a thud when it lands.
WhoThe other team (everyone, in a free-for-all). Teammates are not announced.

Why: it is the sound a shooter uses to find an opponent, and it rewards the quiet play it asks for (crouch to creep up on someone). Turn it off in ⚙ → ENEMY FOOTSTEPS if it is too busy.

🧱 Worn-down maps

The maps were too perfect: clean walls, clean floors, every edge square, every surface one flat colour. Every map now gets a wear pass the first time it is played (after its dressing, with the other upgrades), and it is all detail, never geometry you can hit: no collider, nothing you can stand on or shoot, no change to any route. It is made of two batches per map (one draw call each):

  • Decals on the real faces of the real boxes and on the floor: dirt that gathers at the foot of every wall (and moss, or dust drifted against it, depending on the place), rain streaks and rust runs from the top, plaster and paint that have come away in ragged patches (a pale, rough patch with a shadow behind it), square repair patches in a different tone, cracks that wander and fork, stains, big soft blotches of a different tone that break a wall or floor up from across the map, scorch marks and bullet holes, scuffs, damp patches, and patched bits of floor.
  • Rubble: small loose chunks heaped at the foot of walls and scattered on the floor.

How much, and in what dirt, depends on the place: a trench, the sewer or Chernobyl are filthy (soot, rust, moss), urban streets are cracked and chipped and the desert is dusty and flaking. Places that are not meant to look used are left clean: the neon and sci-fi maps (Cyber City, Studio, Dreamscape, the space station, the labs, the vault), the fairground ones (Carnival, Holiday, Traffic Cone Republic), the opera house and the supermarket — rust and soot on a hard-light city read as a different map. So are the 1v1 duel arenas (plain grid, flat colours), the shooting range, Lobby 13 and the pinball arcade. On a dark wall dirt would not show, so there the wear is the pale kind (dust, lime, scuffed paint). Everything is seeded by the map’s name, so everybody’s copy is worn the same way, and the map-vote screenshots show it.

Why: a place that has been fought over, lived in and rained on looks like one, and the eye finds a flat, square, clean surface instantly: it is the quickest tell that something is a 3D model. Nothing here changes how a map plays.

🎲 The RNG pool

Dice Duel and Weapon Roulette deal from one pool, by tier, and it holds three kinds of thing: guns, melees and utilities. Most of it is the retired gimmick weapons: items taken out of the shop and the loadout because they are too odd, too strong or too silly for a normal match (Potato Cannon, Portal Launcher, Pinball Launcher, Lightsabre, Orbital Strike, Hunter Drone, Rubber Duck...) are exactly what a dice roll is for, so they come up in these modes and nowhere else. The tier is how good the thing is in a duel against a gun, so melees and most utilities sit low: rolling a Frying Pan is a bad day, which is the point. Every tier holds all three kinds, and a roll is a melee or a utility about one time in five. Weapon Roulette deals you a gun 70% of the time, a melee 15% and a utility 15% (a new one every 20 seconds into the same two-slot kit), and bots in either mode can only fight with guns. Retired items come up a quarter as often as the other things in their tier, so the odd leftovers stay a surprise and the everyday guns stay the usual roll. The one admin item in the pool is the Ω Omega Pistol (SS tier): one bullet that kills whatever it hits, and the one way anybody but an admin gets to fire it. It is yours alone: a bot is never dealt it. The test (verify-boot.js --rng) puts every item in the pool in your hands, uses it and gives your own kit back, and counts how often the Omega Pistol and the retired items turn up.

🗺️ Voting for the map

The map used to be chosen on the mode screen before anyone was found, and only 1v1 had a vote. Now there is no picking up front: every lobby match asks once the people are known. Each human votes for a map from a screenshot of it (taken from a corner, about three-quarters, through a first-person lens), the map with the most votes plays and a tie is a coin flip. 1v1 chooses among the four duel maps; 2v2 among the whole pool and the four duel maps; everything else among the whole pool, in a scrolling grid. A bot only votes when it is your opponent in a 1v1; in any other mode only people do, so alone against bots your pick is final. Nobody voting in 15–25 seconds is a random map. King of the Hill and the M4 towers play their own arena and skip the vote. A mode with nobody to vote with (Dice Duel, Weapon Roulette, Pie Fight: you and bots) shows the same screen to you alone, with a RANDOM button.

Why: a map chosen before you know who you are fighting is a guess; chosen after, it is a negotiation, and a picture of the place says more than its name. The screenshots are made by tools/render-map-previews.js, which loads every map in a headless browser and picks the best of about 48 cameras for each (not facing a wall, lit, plenty to see); run it again when a map changes.

📱 Touch controls

Phone players said two things: the buttons were too small, and some did not work. Both were true. The JUMP button set a flag that only an old, retired piece of the jump code read, so tapping it launched no one; and SLIDE only started if the stick was already being held, but the thumb that holds the stick is not the one that reaches the button.

WhatNow
SizeFIRE is 96 px (was 72); every other button is at least 56 px (they were 44–48, smaller than a fingertip), with a little invisible slop around each so a near miss still counts.
JUMPUses the same jump as the Space key: slide-jump, crouch-jump and the double jump all work. A tap that cannot be used no longer waits and fires when you land.
SLIDEMoved next to JUMP, under the right thumb. One tap slides the way the stick was last pushed (if that was a moment ago), otherwise straight ahead; the old rule still held the Shift key to “moving only”. A bladed weapon's air dash works from the button too.
CROUCHA toggle (tap down, tap up); JUMP also stands you up and dying clears it. It can be set back to hold-to-crouch.

⚙ → TOUCH CONTROLS (the CONTROLS button on a phone, where there are no keys to rebind) has sliders for button size (0.7–1.5×, held to what the screen can fit), button opacity, joystick size and look sensitivity — the window fades while a slider is held so you can watch the buttons change — and MOVE BUTTONS, which lets you drag every button (including the ability and SWAP ones that only appear when needed) wherever your hands are. Positions are saved on the device, separately for landscape and portrait, and are pulled back on screen if the screen is smaller than the one they were set on. RESET TO DEFAULT undoes all of it.

Why: a layout that does not fit the hand is the first thing a new phone player runs into, and a button that does nothing teaches them the game is broken. Letting them place the buttons is the fix that works for every hand and every phone.

🧭 How the bots get round walls

Bots used to steer straight at you and feel their way round whatever was in the way, which was fine for a pillar and not for a long wall: on Duel Grid and Crossroads a bot ran back and forth along the centre divider for a whole minute, and on seven of sixteen older maps (urban, airport, trenches, studio, supermarket, labyrinth, sewer) a bot sent across the map simply did not arrive. When the straight line to you is blocked at kerb height a bot now follows a route over a grid of what a body can walk (every bot shares one fill from your position, rebuilt as you move or as cover is destroyed): it cuts across open floor and only turns at corners, keeps a body's width off them, treats anything taller than a kerb as a wall (a ramp's own steps included), steers clear of damage zones, and if it finds itself caught it goes cell by cell for a few seconds. It still falls back to the old steering wherever it has no route. The grid is 1.5 m wherever it can be (a thin wall can never fall between its cells; on Urban they used to, and a bot walked straight into the wall and stayed there). In a test of one bot sent across each map, 64 of 64 runs on eight maps (the four duel maps, Urban, Trenches, Airport, Supermarket) arrived.

🤖 Who the bots shoot

In team fights (1v1 to 3v3 rounds, 5v5, 10v10 and Frontlines) every bot, on either side, goes for the nearest opponent: your allies are as much a target for the enemy as you are. It used to be that the enemy bots all ignored your teammates and walked straight at you, while those teammates, whose shots at other bots always land, shot them in the back; so the player's side won nearly every match, by a lot. Base Raid is the exception: its guards are there for you.

🏛️ Lobby System

Every multiplayer mode routes you through a staging lobby. Players ready up, and the undermanned side can vote to fill with bots or play short-handed. Each match runs in its own isolated bubble — concurrent games don't bleed into each other.

🧍 Characters

Everyone is built out of hard-edged blocks wearing a pixel texture, the way a Minecraft character is. They used to be bevelled boxes with a face drawn on: brows, a nose, cheeks and a smile, softened at every edge. At the size a player actually sees another player, that reads as a plush toy rather than a character, and the nose was three brown pixels that looked like damage.

Every character texture is a 16×16 grid of whole pixels, magnified with no smoothing and no mipmaps. That rule is the whole look — one gradient or one half-pixel edge and the character stops reading as pixel art and starts reading as a small drawing, which is worse than either. Eyes are deliberately big and low on the face: at three pixels they vanished past a few metres.

Everyone is the same soldier in different colours. The kit that worked was the SWAT one, so it became the template and the default: balaclava under a helmet, a goggle band across the eyes, a plate carrier whose magazine pouches have amber tips, knee pads. The body is identical on every skin — what tells them apart is the head, which is how real kit reads at the distance you actually see another player from.

Six issues. Operator is the default, black on black with grey webbing. Soldier is olive with the goggles pushed up onto the helmet and the face uncovered — the only one in the set you can see is a person. Riot is navy behind a full clear face shield, with the face drawn dimmer through it. Desert Recon is a sand helmet, amber goggles and a shemagh wrapped over the nose and mouth, with no skin showing at all. Night Ops is three blacks and two green tubes, nothing else. Juggernaut is welded plate with a slit, lit red from inside.

The old name-seeded look is still there as Recruit, and the accessory skins (Shadow, Spiky, Green Cap, Riot Chad) keep working. Sizes, joints and hitboxes did not move by a millimetre, so animation and shooting cannot tell which skin is on.

🎨 Skins

Skins are purely cosmetic. Every one of them changes how a weapon looks — body and accent colours, the tint of its tracer, sometimes a effect shell like crystal or smoke — and none of them changes damage, spread, fire rate, magazine, reload or anything else that decides a fight. A gold AK kills exactly as fast as a cardboard one.

That is enforced in code rather than by discipline: a skin's stats are filtered down to the cosmetic keys before they ever reach the weapon, so a skin that declared a damage number would simply be ignored.

There are three kinds. Colour themes (22) repaint every gun at once. Model skins replace the weapon with a different object entirely — 91 of them across 72 guns, from the AUG to a leaf blower, a slushie machine, a telescope on the SR-X, a banana Desert Eagle, an accordion MG42, a boombox for a shockwave launcher, a toaster for twin barrels and a tuba where the bazooka was — and they keep the weapon's stats, its reload track and its prop beats, so nothing about the fight changes. Melee model skins do the same for the other hand: 30 of them — every melee you can actually equip now has an alternative, admin blades included. The knife becomes a floss dispenser, the fire axe becomes an actual electric guitar (it is called an axe), the riot shield becomes a trash can lid, the lightsabre becomes a fluorescent tube, the Vampire Blade becomes a ketchup bottle, the Volt Whip becomes a string of Christmas lights and the Gravity Hammer becomes a meat tenderizer. On the admin rack the Karambit becomes a can opener, the Trench Bayonet a cocktail toothpick, the Tomahawk an ice scraper, the OTs-04 a nail file and the Spec-Ops Garrote a phone charger. Same damage, same reach, same ability.

One family goes further than a different object. The emoticon skins are guns drawn out of characters — the text gun ᵕᠵデᡁ╼━ stretched into three dimensions, white faces with black rims and nothing in between. There are five of them, on the AK-20, pistol, SR-X, SG8 and minigun, and they fire -. The dash is the only thing in the game a skin changes about a projectile, it is still purely cosmetic, and it only applies to rounds YOU fire: an enemy holding the same weapon is holding their own skin, and the server is never told about any of it.

Every gun skin has its own sound, and every object has its own reload. A barcode scanner beeps and takes a battery; a glue gun takes a glue stick in the back; the crisp tube is refilled with crisps; the champagne is shaken and re-corked; the egg timer is wound; the pipe organ has its bellows pumped; the emoticon guns are backspaced and retyped. Skins that are real guns — the AUG, the Dragunov, the M249 — keep their gun’s reload, because they take the same ammunition, but each has its own voice. All of it is heard only by you: other players hear your gun, not your skin.

Everything you draw is pulled up from the hip, and it takes time. A weapon swings up from off-screen bottom-right as one rigid object, and until it is up you cannot shoot, swing, throw, reload or use its ability (a thin bar under the crosshair drains while you wait; the quick ones show none). The time depends on what it is, from the table in the game: sidearms take 0.3–0.5 s (the pistol is the quickest gun at 0.3, the hand cannon the slowest sidearm at 0.5), small blades are nearly instant (fists 0.10, knife 0.12, katana 0.17), SMGs about 0.52–0.56 s, an AK-20 0.7 s, rifles and shotguns 0.65–0.9 s, launchers and machine guns about a second to 1.25 s, and the minigun 1.5 s, the slowest and the cap: nothing takes longer. A big melee is in between (a sledge 0.52, a chainsaw 0.6). Pressing the key for what you are already holding costs nothing, quick melee (B) takes no time, there or back, and a Dice Duel roll is its own wait. The old weight table could not say which gun is heavy (every sidearm and utility is the same 0.05, and the minigun shares a 0.30 with the sledge), so this is a table by weapon, with a default per slot for anything new.

Some skins make a better entrance. Draw the Hyperspace AK and it arrives as crystal shards hanging in space around your hands, which then fuse into a rifle. The Origami Pistol folds itself up out of a sheet of paper, the Brick XM7 is snapped together a brick at a time, the 8-Bit SR-X pops in a pixel at a time, and the Portal SG8 steps out of a portal. It is only something to look at: fire, reload or aim and the gun is instantly whole, so an entrance can never make you slower to shoot.

The Hyperspace Knife fuses from crystal like the AK; the Butterfly Knife’s handles flip round the blade and close as the grip; the Laser Katana’s blade runs out of the hilt; the Frost Axe is tossed up and caught; the Outlaw Shorty spin-cocks round its loop lever; and the Gunslinger twirls twice round the trigger finger. Swing a blade or fire a gun and it is instantly ready.
